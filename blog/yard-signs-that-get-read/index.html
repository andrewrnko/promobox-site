Yard signor eye test?

A yard sign gets a glance from a moving car. How to pick the one line that matters, the right size and the right material.

Most yard signs try to say too much. A driver going past a lawn gets a glance. If the sign reads like a flyer, it reads like nothing. Here is how to make one that works from the road.

Start where the viewer stands

A sign beside a walkway gets read differently than one across a road. Before you write a word, think about where the sign goes and how far away people will be. Close up, a short web address can work. From a moving car, people catch the business name and maybe the service.

One line wins

Pick one line to be the biggest thing on the sign. Usually it is the business name or the service. If every line is the same size, the viewer has to decide what matters, and they will not bother.

  1. 1

    Main line

    Business name or service, as big as the sign allows.

  2. 2

    Second line

    One short supporting line, like the service area or the trade.

  3. 3

    Contact

    One way to reach you. A phone number or a short web address. Not both, unless there is room.

Shop note

When the layout feels crowded, cut a line before you shrink everything. Making all the text smaller leaves the whole sign harder to read.

Do the small-preview check

Shrink the proof down on your screen and show it to someone who has not seen it. Ask what they noticed first and what the business does. If they miss it, fix that. This tests the hierarchy, not the final size, so check real dimensions too.

Sizes and material

The most common yard sign sizes are 18 by 24 inches and 24 by 36 inches. Roadside signs usually need the bigger one. We print custom sizes too. Yard signs are corrugated plastic, single or double-sided, with stakes.

Tell the shop how long the sign has to work. Corrugated plastic is right for short runs and campaigns. For a permanent flat sign outside, aluminum composite, or ACM, is usually the better material. Same artwork, different job.

Track the calls

Yard signs can be a sales tool you measure. One of our Puget Sound customers prints a different phone number on each city's batch of signs, so they know which batch makes the phone ring. Even a simple version, like asking every caller where they saw you, tells you if the signs are working.
